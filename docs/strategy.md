# How we approached the Coda brief

The module asked a narrow question. Can a small, ethical roaster like Coda Coffee gain from a mobile app that is built to help people rather than to raise revenue, and does giving up short-term sales make it easier or harder to compete? Our answer is Cup2Farm: a code on every Coda cup opens a web app that shows the lot's verified journey and proof the farmers were paid, then carries a thank-you, feedback or a tip back to the growers.

Our strategy starts from the problem in the case, not from a technology. More than 95 percent of Coda's business is wholesale. It earns trust at origin through Farm2Cup, its B Corp status and the Bext360 pilot in Uganda, but that trust stops at the cafe door, because most people who drink Coda never learn whose coffee it is. The case ends by asking how Coda can sell the value of the Bext360 partnership to its customers. We chose one benevolent area of the future app that answers that question, and left ordering and loyalty to other areas on purpose. Farm2Cup moves coffee and proof down the chain; Cup2Farm sends something back up it.

Our approach was to build on what already runs. Bext360's machine vision grades the cherries, its blockchain records each step, and farmers are paid instantly. We add only a code, a web page and a return path. Several choices follow from the lecture. It is a web app, so there is nothing to download and no account, and no app-store gatekeeper stands between Coda and the drinker; those gatekeepers are the vendor data islands the lecture describes. AI stays where it already works, in grading at origin, and we do not dress the rest up as AI, which is the AI washing the lecture warns about. We collect six fields from drinkers, each tied to something they chose to do: the lot scanned, the venue code, a note, a tip, a rating and an email to get the growers' reply. We do not track location or sell anything. The lecture's line that trust plus value earns access to data is the reason, and so is its warning that a collect-everything culture leaves companies with data they cannot use. We then showed the idea working in a phone prototype with sample data, tied each screen to the source that feeds it, and built a small ROI model that admits the first year does not pay for itself and the third only just clears break-even.

The main theory is Urban and Sultan's case for benevolent apps. They place apps on a spectrum from product push to benevolent service and argue that trust rests on competence, confidence and benevolence. Coda already has the first two on record; the app supplies the third. Their Liberty Mutual study, where a benevolent app raised consideration by 24 percent and purchase likelihood by 14 percent, is our evidence that benevolence can pay, and their steps (design a light version, test it with users, launch with marketing support) shape our rollout. We also borrowed their survey design to measure trust afterwards, so the result rests on a comparison with a control group rather than on a correlation, which is the lecture's point about causation.

The second theory is Hagiu and Wright's test of when data creates a competitive advantage. We scored Cup2Farm against their seven questions without flattery. Scan data on its own is not a moat, since a code and a trace page are becoming standard as regulation makes traceability normal. What is hard to copy is Coda's proprietary origin records, its proof of payment and its network of growers, co-ops and cafes. That is why we frame the code as infrastructure and the two-way channel as the innovation.

From the lecture we also used the algorithmic experience wheel of intent, interaction and identity. Most apps turn that loop toward the company and keep users with reward loops. We point it at the drinker's own interest in where the coffee came from, and we let it end when the cup does: no points, no streaks, and return visits only when something real happens, such as a new lot or a reply from the growers. This follows the lecture's advice to move away from coupons and toward a lasting relationship. Finally, the three kinds of analytics give Coda a use for the feedback: descriptive reports on which lots and cafes people engage with, predictive signals for sourcing and demand, and prescriptive choices about where to invest at origin, with the same feedback going back to the farmers.

In short, we answered the brief with one feature that sells nothing, uses data Coda already has, asks drinkers for very little, and turns Coda's work at origin into trust that the people drinking its coffee can see. The advantage comes from relationships and records that a rival cannot copy, not from the app itself.
